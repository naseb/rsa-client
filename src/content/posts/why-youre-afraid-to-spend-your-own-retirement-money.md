A financial planner I read about recently put it perfectly: most of his clients didn't come to him with a savings problem. They came to him with a *spending* problem. They had the money. They just couldn't make themselves use it.

If that sounds familiar — if you've got a number in your account that should feel like freedom but instead feels like a thing you're terrified to touch — you're not broken, and you're not alone. There's actually a name for what you're feeling, real research behind why it happens, and a better way to plan around it than the advice you've probably already heard.

---

## 1. You're Not Imagining It — Most Retirees Feel This Exact Fear

In a 2025 study, Allianz found that **64% of Americans are more afraid of running out of money than they are of dying.** Read that again. Not "worried about." More afraid of running out of money than of death itself.

It even has a clinical-sounding name now: **FORO — Fear of Running Out.** Unlike FOMO, which makes you chase things you don't have, FORO makes you white-knuckle the things you do have. Financial advisors have also coined a darker, more colorful term for the extreme version: **"bag lady syndrome"** — the irrational conviction that you're one bad year away from destitution, no matter how big your portfolio actually is.

Here's the part that should make you feel better, not worse: this fear is *not* correlated with how much money you have. Advisors regularly describe two kinds of clients sitting across the same desk — someone with $5 million who's terrified to retire, and someone with $300,000 who sleeps just fine. The dollar amount isn't the variable. The plan is.

**Why this happens to so many of us:**

- **You spent 30–40 years being rewarded for not spending.** Every paycheck, the habit was: save first, spend what's left. Retirement flips that completely — there's no new paycheck, and suddenly the "right" move is to *draw down* the thing you spent decades protecting. Psychologists call this the shift from "accumulation mode" to "decumulation mode," and for most people it's the single hardest mental adjustment in retirement, harder than the financial math itself.
- **Spending used to feel earned. Now it feels like loss.** When you were working, a withdrawal request was just a normal Tuesday. In retirement, every withdrawal visibly shrinks a number that isn't being refilled. Your brain reads that as danger, even when your spreadsheet says otherwise.
- **It's evolutionary, not irrational.** Humans who hoarded resources during uncertainty survived; humans who didn't, often didn't. You're not weak for feeling this — you're running ancient hardware in a situation it was never built for (a 30-year retirement funded by a portfolio, rather than tomorrow's harvest).

And the cost of this fear is real. Research consistently finds that retirees who lock in a "never touch the principal" mentality end up living **needlessly small lives** — skipping the trip, declining to help a grandchild, buying the generic version of everything — not because they can't afford otherwise, but because no one ever gave them a number they could actually trust.

That's really the core problem. It's not that people don't want to enjoy their retirement. It's that almost nobody has a plan precise enough to say, with confidence, *"Yes — this specific amount, this year, is safe to spend."* Without that number, fear fills the gap.

## 2. Why the 4% Rule Doesn't Actually Solve This

If you've researched retirement spending at all, you've run into "the 4% rule" — withdraw 4% of your portfolio in year one, then increase that dollar amount with inflation every year after, and historically your money should last 30 years.

It's simple, it's memorable, and for thirty years it was "good enough." But it was never designed to *calm* your fear of running out — it was designed to survive the worst historical case, which is a very different goal.

A few things the 4% rule doesn't account for:

- **It assumes you spend the exact same amount, every single year, for 30 years.** No big trip in year one. No pulling back in a bad market year. No tapering off as you naturally slow down in your 70s and 80s. Real life doesn't work that way, and pretending it does is part of why the number feels untrustworthy.
- **It ignores the order your returns show up in** — what's known as *sequence of returns risk*. A market downturn that hits in your first year or two of retirement does dramatically more damage than the same downturn ten years in, because you're withdrawing from an already-shrunken balance. The math of "average return over 30 years" can be true and still hide a brutal first five years.
- **It was built as a worst-case safety net, not a spending guide.** Researchers have pointed out that the 4% rule was deliberately conservative — designed so you wouldn't run out even in the worst market conditions on record. Most retirees aren't living through the worst-case scenario, which means a strict 4% rule frequently tells people to underspend relative to what they could actually, safely afford.
- **It treats every dollar the same**, regardless of whether it's sitting in a pre-tax 401(k), a Roth IRA, or a regular brokerage account — even though each of those is taxed completely differently the moment you touch it.

None of this means the 4% rule is "wrong." It means it answers a narrower question than the one most people are actually asking. "Will I technically survive 30 years on this withdrawal rate?" is not the same question as "How much can I actually enjoy spending, this year, without risking the years after it?"

## 3. The Research-Backed Alternative: Retirement Spending Isn't Flat — It's a Smile

Here's something that should genuinely change how you think about your number. Researcher David Blanchett studied real household spending data and found that retiree spending doesn't follow a flat line at all. It follows a curve that's become known in financial planning circles as the **"retirement spending smile."**

The pattern looks like this:

```text
Spending
 High |  \                                  ___
      |   \                              ___
      |    \____                    ____/
      |         \________________/
  Low |        (the "smile" dips, then rises)
      +------------------------------------------> Age
        Early retirement   Mid-retirement   Late retirement
        (active, social)   (settled, calmer)  (healthcare-driven)
```

- **Early retirement ("Go-Go" years):** Spending is highest. You're healthy, active, and finally have time for travel, hobbies, and the things you put off for decades.
- **Mid-retirement ("Slow-Go" years):** Spending naturally tapers. The pace slows, the big trips become less frequent, and day-to-day costs settle into a lower, steadier rhythm.
- **Late retirement ("No-Go" years):** Spending often ticks back *up* — not for travel, but because healthcare and care-related costs rise, sometimes significantly, in your 80s and beyond.

The practical implication is bigger than it sounds: because most retirement calculators assume flat, inflation-adjusted spending for 30 straight years, they tend to **overstate how much you actually need** — by as much as 15–25%, according to some of the research built on this pattern. That's not a rounding error. That's the difference between working three more years you didn't need to work, and retiring on schedule.

This also reframes the whole "how much can I spend" question. The right answer usually isn't one flat number for the rest of your life — it's a *front-loaded* number that's higher while you're active, and naturally lower later, with a buffer built back in for the healthcare years at the end.

## 4. What About Right Now? Markets Are Jumpy and You're About to Retire

If you're reading this within a few years of retiring, there's a very legitimate, very current version of this fear: *what if the market drops the moment I stop working?*

This isn't an irrational worry — it's the sequence of returns risk mentioned above, and it's mathematically real. Picture retiring with $1 million and taking out $50,000 a year. If the market drops 20% in year one before you've withdrawn anything, you're suddenly working with $800,000 — and then you still take your $50,000 out on top of that. That combination of "lower balance" plus "still withdrawing" can leave a permanent dent that a later market recovery never fully erases, even if the average return over the next 30 years looks perfectly fine on paper.

The retirees who weather this well generally aren't the ones who guessed right about the market. They're the ones who built a plan that doesn't *require* guessing right:

- **Keep a cash buffer for the early years.** Many planners suggest one to three years of living expenses set aside in cash or short-term, low-volatility holdings — money you can live on without selling stocks at a loss if the market drops right as you retire.
- **Know your number before you need it.** If you already know your sustainable spending level was calculated *with* the possibility of a bad first few years in mind, a downturn becomes a data point, not a panic event.
- **Build in flexibility, not rigidity.** A plan that can absorb "spend a little less this year" without falling apart is fundamentally more resilient than a flat, locked-in number — and far less stressful to live with.

Not a better mood. Not "just trust the math." A plan specific enough that you stop having to guess.

The retirees who genuinely relax into spending their money tend to share one thing in common: they have a number they trust, built from *their* actual accounts, *their* actual Social Security timing, *their* actual tax situation — not a generic rule of thumb borrowed from someone else's retirement.
